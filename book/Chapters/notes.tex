\section{Phrasal Verbs}

A phrasal verb is a combination of a verb and one or more particles (prepositions or adverbs) that together create a meaning different from the original verb. Phrasal verbs are commonly used in everyday English and can be separable or inseparable.


\subsection{Common Phrasal Verbs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Look up:} To search for information (e.g., in a dictionary or online).
    \item \textbf{Give up:} To stop trying or to quit.
    \item \textbf{Take off:} To remove clothing or to leave the ground (for airplanes).
    \item \textbf{Run out of:} To have no more of something left.
    \item \textbf{Break down:} To stop functioning (for machines) or to become emotionally upset.
    \item \textbf{Put off:} To postpone or delay.
    \item \textbf{Get along with:} To have a good relationship with someone.
    \item \textbf{Turn on/off:} To activate or deactivate a device.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When to Use Rephrasal?}

Rephrasal is the act of expressing the same idea using different words or phrases. It is often used to avoid repetition, clarify meaning, or simplify complex ideas.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Explain with others word for someone understand better
    \item Avoid repeating the same Words
    \item Make your writing more interesting
    \item Simplification of complex ideas
    \item Clarify meaning
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When to Use Paraphrasal?}

Paraphrasal is the act of rephrasing or restating a sentence or passage using different words while maintaining the original meaning. It is often used in academic writing, summarizing, and note-taking.

\begin{itemize}
    \item To summarize information
    \item To avoid plagiarism
    \item To clarify complex ideas
    \item To understand better
    \item Literature review
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How to Choose Linking Words Cause}


When choosing linking words for cause, consider the following guidelines:

\subsubsection{Because}
Use "because" to introduce a reason. It is followed by a complete clause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Main clause because + subject + verb.
    \item Example: I study because I want to learn.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Due to}
Use "due to" to introduce a cause. It is followed by a noun or noun phrase.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Due to + noun phrase.
    \item Example: The cancellation was due to the storm.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{How to Choose Linking Words Effect}


When choosing linking words for effect, consider the following guidelines:

\subsubsection{Therefore}
Use "therefore" to introduce a result. It is often used to connect two independent clauses or sentences.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A. Therefore, Sentence B. / Sentence A; therefore, Sentence B.
    \item Example: It was raining, therefore we stayed inside.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{As a result}
Use "as a result" to describe the consequence of an action or situation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A. As a result, Sentence B.
    \item Example: He missed the bus; as a result, he was late.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Consequently}
Use "consequently" to show that something happens because of the previous statement.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A; consequently, Sentence B.
    \item Example: He didn't sleep; consequently, he is tired.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{How to Choose Linking Words Contrast}


When choosing linking words for contrast, consider the following guidelines:

\subsubsection{However}
Use "however" to introduce a statement that contrasts with or contradicts the previous statement. It is often used at the beginning of a sentence or clause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A. However, Sentence B. / Sentence A; however, Sentence B.
    \item Example: I wanted to go for a walk; however, it started raining.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Whereas}
Use "whereas" to highlight a contrast between two clauses or ideas. It is typically used within a sentence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Clause A, whereas Clause B.
    \item Example: She loves hiking, whereas he prefers swimming.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{On the other hand}
Use "on the other hand" to present an alternative perspective or contrasting idea. It is often used to introduce a different point of view.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A. On the other hand, Sentence B.
    \item Example: The new policy is cost-effective. On the other hand, it may lead to job losses.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Although}
Use "although" when you want to introduce a contrast between two clauses. It is followed by a subject and verb.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Although + subject + verb, main clause.
    \item Example: Although it was raining, we went for a walk.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{In spite of the fact that}
Use "in spite of the fact that" to introduce a contrast between two clauses. It is followed by a subject and verb.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: In spite of the fact that + subject + verb, main clause.
    \item Example: In spite of the fact that it was raining, we went for a walk.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Nevertheless}
Use "nevertheless" to introduce a statement that contrasts with or contradicts the previous statement. It is often used at the beginning of a sentence or clause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A. Nevertheless, Sentence B. / Sentence A; nevertheless, Sentence B.
    \item Example: The task was difficult; nevertheless, she completed it.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{While}
Use "while" to highlight a contrast between two clauses or ideas. It is typically used within a sentence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Clause A, while Clause B.
    \item Example: He enjoys hiking, while she prefers swimming.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Despite}
Use "despite" when you want to show that one thing happened even though there was a contrasting factor. It is followed by a noun or gerund.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Despite + noun / gerund, main clause.
    \item Example: Despite the rain, we went for a walk.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Yet}
Use "yet" to introduce a statement that contrasts with or contradicts the previous statement. It is often used in the middle of a sentence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Form: Sentence A, yet Sentence B.
    \item Example: It was raining, yet they went for a walk.
\end{itemize}

\section{Skill vs Talent}

\textbf{Skill} refers to the ability to perform a task or activity effectively, often acquired through practice, training, or experience. Skills can be developed and improved over time.

\textbf{Talent}, on the other hand, refers to a natural aptitude or innate ability to excel in a particular area or activity. Talent is often seen as something one is born with and may not require as much effort to develop compared to skills.

\subsection{Examples of Skill}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Playing a musical instrument
    \item Speaking a foreign language
    \item Cooking
    \item Writing
    \item Programming
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Examples of Talent}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Artistic ability (e.g., painting, drawing)
    \item Athletic ability (e.g., running, jumping)
    \item Musical ability (e.g., perfect pitch)
    \item Mathematical ability
    \item Leadership ability
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Differences}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acquisition:} Skills are learned and developed through practice, while talents are innate and natural.
    \item \textbf{Improvement:} Skills can be improved with effort and training, whereas talents may not require as much effort to excel.
    \item \textbf{Scope:} Skills are often specific to certain tasks or activities, while talents can be broader and apply to multiple areas.
\end{itemize}

\section{Modals of Probability and Advice}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Should:} Is often used to describe the ideal or best thing to do. \\
    \textit{Example: There should be many more green jobs.}
    \item \textbf{Ought to be:} Is used to describe something we want to happen. You can add `really' for emphasis. \\
    \textit{Example: Governments really ought to tax the big polluters.}
    \item \textbf{Must:} Is used to describe something we see as necessary. \\
    \textit{Example: There must be global action on climate change now.}
    \item \textbf{Can:} Is used to talk about general truths and tendencies. \\
    \textit{Example: As a nation we can provide better jobs for younger people.}
    \item \textbf{Should be + `-ing':} Is used to describe something we expect to happen. \\
    \textit{Example: Global temperatures should be falling in the next 15 years.}
    \item \textbf{May:} Can be used with a selection of adverbs such as `never', `just' or `only'. \\
    \textit{Example: We may only see the results of our climate change initiatives in 20 years.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Past Tenses}

The \textbf{Past Perfect} and \textbf{Past Perfect Continuous} are both used to talk about actions that happened before a specific time in the past, but they focus on different aspects of the action.

\subsection{Past Simple}

The \textbf{Past Simple} is used to describe actions that were completed at a specific time in the past.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structure:} Subject + verb (past form)
    \item \textbf{Focus:} Action completed in the past.
    \item \textbf{Example:} I \textbf{saw} a movie yesterday.
    \item \textbf{Example:} She \textbf{arrived} late.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Past Perfect}

The \textbf{Past Perfect} (had + past participle) is used to emphasize the \textbf{completion} or \textbf{result} of an action that happened before another action in the past.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structure:} Subject + had + past participle
    \item \textbf{Focus:} Completed action, result.
    \item \textbf{Example:} By the time I arrived, she \textbf{had left} (the action was finished).
    \item \textbf{Example:} I \textbf{had finished} my homework before the movie started.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Past Perfect Continuous}

The \textbf{Past Perfect Continuous} (had + been + verb-ing) is used to emphasize the \textbf{duration} or \textbf{ongoing nature} of an action that was happening up until another past action or time.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structure:} Subject + had + been + verb-ing
    \item \textbf{Focus:} Duration, ongoing activity, cause of a past feeling/state.
    \item \textbf{Example:} She was tired because she \textbf{had been running} for an hour.
    \item \textbf{Example:} It \textbf{had been raining} all night, so the ground was wet.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Differences}

\begin{longtable}{|p{4.5cm}|p{4.5cm}|p{4.5cm}|}
    \hline
    \textbf{Past Simple} & \textbf{Past Perfect} & \textbf{Past Perfect Continuous} \\ \hline
    \endhead
    Focuses on completed actions at a specific time. & Focuses on the \textbf{result} or completion. & Focuses on the \textbf{process} or duration. \\ \hline
    Refers to single or repeated finished actions. & Refers to \textbf{states} (with stative verbs) or finished actions. & Refers to \textbf{ongoing actions} over a period of time. \\ \hline
    Answers ``When?'' & Answers ``How much/many/often?'' & Answers ``How long?'' \\ \hline
    \textit{Examples:} & \textit{Examples:} & \textit{Examples:} \\
    I wrote three emails yesterday. & I had written three emails. & I had been writing emails for two hours. \\
    She knew him for years. & She had known him for years. (Stative verb) & She had been waiting for him since noon. \\ \hline
\end{longtable}
